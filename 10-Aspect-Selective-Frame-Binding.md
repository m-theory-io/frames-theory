# Aspect-Selective Frame-Binding: Measurement-Like Propagation Across Informational Neighborhoods

William J. House

## Abstract

Frames Theory treats observation as directed information transfer between bounded informational frames. Earlier papers developed this idea through frame-binding, state update, decoherence, redundant objectivity, causal boundaries, and records. This paper drills into a more local question: when a measurement-like event propagates into a neighborhood of frames, why do different frames receive different aspects of the same event? A chair in a room may be physically updated by acoustic vibration from an approaching ice cream truck. A person in the same room may be updated by those same vibrations, but also by higher-order perceptual, semantic, and aesthetic information: that an ice cream truck is approaching, that the melody is familiar, cheerful, irritating, nostalgic, or musically structured. The event is shared, but the uptake is not identical.

The central claim of this paper is that measurement-like events propagate as structured bundles of informational aspects, and each receiving frame binds only those aspects compatible with its state-space, coupling channels, memory, and interpretive constraints. This process is called aspect-selective frame-binding. Exposure is not binding; binding is not interpretation; interpretation is not objectivity. A receiving frame's class or type determines which propagated aspects are admissible for that frame. The result is a layered account of information propagation in which physical, thermodynamic, perceptual, semantic, and aesthetic uptake are not separate worlds, but different levels of frame-compatible binding to the same originating event.

## 1. Introduction

Frames Theory begins with the claim that physical description is always situated within bounded informational contexts. A frame receives, stores, transforms, or constrains information through interaction. Observation is not restricted to conscious awareness. It is a directed information-transfer relation between frames. Measurement is a special case in which such transfer creates a stable record.

The previous papers in this sequence developed several consequences of that view. A frame may bind to an outcome. A state update is relative to the receiving frame's accessible information. Classical objectivity emerges when records become redundantly stabilized across many compatible frames. Relativistic and cosmological settings impose causal information boundaries. Time and memory arise from ordered update and record accessibility.

This paper asks a smaller but important question:

> When an event propagates through a local environment, what determines which aspects of that event a given frame can receive?

The question is easy to miss because ordinary language often treats events as single things. We say, "the ice cream truck passed the house," or "the music entered the room." But physically and informationally, the event is not delivered as a single undifferentiated object. It propagates through multiple channels and at multiple levels of organization: pressure waves, vibrations, thermal perturbations, spatial motion, patterned melody, source identification, social meaning, and aesthetic valence.

Different frames in the same neighborhood do not bind all of these aspects. A wooden chair may resonate with the sound waves. It may absorb tiny amounts of vibrational and thermal energy. It may acquire microscopic physical traces of the event. But it does not bind the event as music, as an approaching truck, or as a nostalgic summer signal. A person standing beside the chair may bind many of those higher-order aspects because the person's frame includes auditory organs, neural processing, memory, learned categories, anticipatory models, and affective valuation.

The chair and the person are therefore not simply two observers with different opinions. They are different receiving frames with different admissible aspect sets. The same originating event updates them differently because their structures define different filters of reception.

The central thesis of this paper is:

> Measurement-like events propagate through informational neighborhoods as structured aspect bundles, and receiving frames bind only those aspects admissible under their physical, functional, mnemonic, and interpretive constraints.

This is aspect-selective frame-binding.

## 2. Measurement-Like Events as Aspect Bundles

In earlier papers, an observation was written schematically as a directed information transfer:

$$
O_{ij}:F_i\rightarrow F_j,
$$

where frame $F_j$ receives information from frame $F_i$.

That notation is useful but coarse. It can make information transfer look like a single payload moving from one frame to another. In ordinary physical situations, however, what propagates from an event is structured. An event may make available many distinct aspects, only some of which are available to a given receiver.

Let an event or generating process be denoted by $E$. Instead of treating $E$ as a monolithic signal, define an associated aspect bundle:

$$
\mathcal{A}(E)=\{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\}.
$$

Each aspect $a_k$ is a distinguishable informational component or level of organization that may be propagated from the event. The aspect bundle is not necessarily finite in a final theory; the notation simply marks that events can be decomposed into frame-relevant informational aspects.

For the ice cream truck event, the aspect bundle may include:

$$
\mathcal{A}(E_{\mathrm{truck}})=
\{
\text{acoustic pressure},
\text{mechanical vibration},
\text{thermal perturbation},
\text{spatial approach},
\text{melodic pattern},
\text{source identity},
\text{social meaning},
\text{aesthetic valence}
\}.
$$

This list is not meant to be exhaustive. It distinguishes levels of uptake. Some aspects are low-level physical couplings. Some are perceptual patterns. Some depend on memory and classification. Some depend on cultural or affective interpretation.

The aspect bundle is therefore layered. Lower-level aspects may be necessary for higher-level aspects without being sufficient for them. Acoustic pressure waves are necessary for hearing the music in the ordinary case, but not sufficient for recognizing the melody or valuing it aesthetically. A microphone may bind acoustic structure without experiencing nostalgia. A person may bind the melody as familiar because the person's frame contains relevant memory records.

This gives Frames Theory a way to discuss local propagation without collapsing all reception into a single category called observation.

## 3. Admissible Aspect Sets

Not every propagated aspect is admissible for every receiving frame. A frame can bind only what it can physically couple to, stabilize, preserve, transform, or interpret under its internal constraints.

For a frame $F_i$ and event $E$, define the admissible aspect set:

$$
\mathcal{A}_i(E)\subseteq \mathcal{A}(E).
$$

$\mathcal{A}_i(E)$ is the subset of propagated event-aspects that $F_i$ can receive in a way that affects its informational state.

The admissible aspect set depends on several factors.

First, it depends on **coupling channels**. A frame must be physically coupled to an aspect in order to receive it. A chair can couple to acoustic vibration through material resonance. A deaf person may still couple to strong vibration through the floor but not bind ordinary airborne sound as auditory melody.

Second, it depends on **state-space capacity**. A receiving frame must have internal degrees of freedom capable of being modified in a relevant way. A surface can record scratches. A detector can record a click. A nervous system can encode auditory patterns. Different state spaces admit different distinctions.

Third, it depends on **stabilization**. A fleeting perturbation may affect a frame without becoming a record. Binding requires enough persistence or constraint to matter for subsequent frame states.

Fourth, it depends on **memory and model structure**. Higher-order aspects require prior records or learned mappings. Recognizing an ice cream truck is not merely hearing frequencies. It requires a mapping from sound pattern and contextual cues to a source category.

Fifth, it depends on **interpretive constraints**. Aesthetic or semantic aspects require frames capable of assigning meaning, value, expectation, or relevance. These are not added from outside physics; they are higher-order update structures in frames with the right organization.

These factors can be summarized schematically:

$$
\mathcal{A}_i(E)
=
\Phi_i\bigl(\mathcal{A}(E);\rho_i,N_i,C_i,R_i\bigr),
$$

where $\Phi_i$ is the frame-specific admissibility filter, $\rho_i$ is the frame state, $N_i$ its accessible neighborhood, $C_i$ its constraints, and $R_i$ its record or memory structure.

This expression is not a new physical law. It is a conceptual scaffold. It says that reception is not determined by the event alone. It is determined by the event together with the receiving frame's structure.

## 4. Exposure, Binding, Interpretation, and Objectivity

Aspect-selective frame-binding requires several distinctions.

**Exposure** occurs when an aspect reaches a frame's boundary or causal neighborhood. The frame is physically in the path of the propagated structure.

**Coupling** occurs when the aspect interacts with degrees of freedom in the frame.

**Binding** occurs when the coupling modifies the receiving frame in a way that stabilizes usable information or constrains later state updates.

**Interpretation** occurs when the bound information is mapped into a higher-order model, category, value, or meaning structure.

**Objectivity** occurs when related aspects are redundantly bound across multiple compatible frames and can be reconciled through shared records.

These are not the same process.

A frame may be exposed without coupling. A wall may block light that never reaches a closed drawer. A frame may couple without binding. A vibration may pass through a material and leave no persistent record. A frame may bind without interpretation. A thermometer may record temperature without understanding weather. A frame may interpret without broad objectivity. A person may hear a melody as nostalgic even if no other nearby frame shares that aesthetic uptake.

The sequence can be written schematically:

$$
\text{exposure}
\rightarrow
\text{coupling}
\rightarrow
\text{binding}
\rightarrow
\text{interpretation}
\rightarrow
\text{objectivity}.
$$

This sequence is not always linear. Some systems bind and interpret recursively. Some records become objective without semantic interpretation by any human. The point is that Frames Theory should not treat all reception as equivalent. Measurement-like propagation can be partial, layered, and frame-specific.

## 5. The Room, the Chair, and the Ice Cream Truck

Consider a room in a residence. Inside the room is a standing person and a chair. Outside the residence, an ice cream truck approaches while playing music. Acoustic waves enter the room through the walls, windows, door gaps, and air. The room itself, the chair, the floor, the person, and other objects become part of the local receiving neighborhood.

Let the originating event be $E_T$: the truck approaching while emitting music. The propagated aspect bundle includes at least:

$$
\mathcal{A}(E_T)=
\{
a_{\mathrm{pressure}},
a_{\mathrm{vibration}},
a_{\mathrm{thermal}},
a_{\mathrm{rhythm}},
a_{\mathrm{melody}},
a_{\mathrm{source}},
a_{\mathrm{approach}},
a_{\mathrm{aesthetic}}
\}.
$$

The chair's admissible aspect set may be approximately:

$$
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{chair}}(E_T)=
\{
a_{\mathrm{pressure}},
a_{\mathrm{vibration}},
a_{\mathrm{thermal}}
\}.
$$

The chair can resonate. It can absorb acoustic energy. It can undergo microscopic thermal and mechanical changes. Depending on material and sensitivity, it may even preserve weak traces. But it does not bind the pattern as melody, infer an approaching source, or assign aesthetic value.

The person's admissible aspect set may be approximately:

$$
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{person}}(E_T)=
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{chair}}(E_T)
\cup
\{
a_{\mathrm{rhythm}},
a_{\mathrm{melody}},
a_{\mathrm{source}},
a_{\mathrm{approach}},
a_{\mathrm{aesthetic}}
\}.
$$

The person receives physical vibration through the body and auditory pressure waves through the ears. Neural processing binds rhythm and melody. Memory and learned environmental categories bind the sound to the source class "ice cream truck." Changes in volume, directionality, and prior experience support the inference that the truck is approaching. Affective and cultural memory may bind the music as pleasant, annoying, nostalgic, childish, commercial, or summery.

The person and the chair therefore share part of the event but not all of it:

$$
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{chair}}(E_T)
\cap
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{person}}(E_T)
\ne
\varnothing,
$$

while

$$
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{chair}}(E_T)
\ne
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{person}}(E_T).
$$

Their overlap is physically real. Their difference is also physically real. It reflects not private subjectivity alone, but distinct admissibility structures in different receiving frames.

## 6. Frame Class and Type

The room example suggests that frames can be grouped by class or type according to what kinds of aspects they can bind.

A **thermodynamic frame** can bind energy exchange, temperature gradients, entropy-producing perturbations, and material state changes.

A **mechanical frame** can bind forces, displacements, stress, pressure, and vibration.

A **sensor frame** can bind selected environmental variables through specialized coupling channels.

A **biological perceptual frame** can bind sensory patterns into organism-relevant state updates.

A **semantic frame** can bind perceptual patterns to categories, concepts, and inferential structures.

An **aesthetic frame** can bind perceptual and semantic patterns to valuation, style, affect, and felt quality.

These classes are not mutually exclusive substances. They are functional organizations of frame capacity. A human person includes thermodynamic, mechanical, sensorimotor, perceptual, semantic, and aesthetic subframes. A chair may include thermodynamic and mechanical frame properties but lacks the organization required for semantic or aesthetic binding.

This allows Frames Theory to distinguish simple physical correlation from richer interpretation without making consciousness a primitive. The theory does not need to say that only conscious observers observe. It can say instead that different frame types bind different aspect classes.

Let $\tau(F_i)$ denote the type or class profile of frame $F_i$. Then admissibility can be written as:

$$
a_k\in\mathcal{A}_i(E)
\quad\text{only if}\quad
a_k\text{ is compatible with }\tau(F_i),\rho_i,C_i,R_i.
$$

This gives a disciplined way to discuss why the chair receives vibration while the person receives music and meaning.

## 7. Semantic and Aesthetic Aspects

The inclusion of semantic and aesthetic aspects may appear to move beyond physics. Frames Theory should treat this carefully.

The claim is not that semantic or aesthetic properties float free of physical processes. Nor is the claim that the chair secretly receives music but fails to report it. The claim is that higher-order aspects are physical-information structures available only to frames with the appropriate organization.

Consider the melody. At one level, it is a pattern in acoustic pressure over time. At another level, it is a sequence of pitch relations. At another, it is a culturally familiar tune associated with ice cream trucks. At another, it may be experienced as cheerful, irritating, nostalgic, or absurd. Each level depends on lower-level physical structure but also requires a receiving frame capable of mapping that structure into the relevant distinction.

Thus, semantic and aesthetic binding can be understood as model-mediated frame update. A frame with suitable memory and interpretive structure maps incoming patterns into categories and values:

$$
a_{\mathrm{melody}}
\xrightarrow{R_i,C_i}
a_{\mathrm{source}},
$$

$$
a_{\mathrm{melody}}
\xrightarrow{R_i,C_i}
a_{\mathrm{aesthetic}}.
$$

The arrows here do not represent external metaphysical additions. They represent transformations internal to the receiving frame. The same acoustic pattern can therefore support different higher-order bindings in different people. One person may bind the tune as nostalgic; another as annoying; another may not recognize it at all. These differences reflect distinct record structures, histories, and interpretive constraints.

This also explains why higher-order aspects can be socially stabilized. If many people in a community recognize the same tune as "ice cream truck music," then the semantic aspect becomes redundantly bound across a human frame network. The source category becomes more objective than a private association because many compatible frames can recover it.

Aesthetic aspects may be less redundant. They can still be real frame-relative updates, but their objectivity depends on how widely and consistently they are shared across relevant frames.

## 8. Neighborhoods of Partial Objectivity

Earlier Frames Theory papers defined objectivity as redundant frame-binding across a network. Aspect-selective binding refines this by showing that objectivity is aspect-relative.

An event may become objective with respect to one aspect but not another.

In the room example, the presence of acoustic vibration may be objective across the chair, the air, the walls, the person's auditory system, and a microphone. The semantic judgment "an ice cream truck is approaching" may be objective across the person, another person, a surveillance camera with recognition software, and later visual confirmation. The aesthetic judgment "the music is charming" may be objective only within a smaller community of frames, or perhaps only within one person's current state.

Thus, objectivity should not be attached only to entire events. It should be attached to aspects:

$$
\mathcal{O}(a_k;\mathcal{N})
$$

where $\mathcal{O}$ denotes the degree of objectivity of aspect $a_k$ within neighborhood $\mathcal{N}$.

A schematic expression might be:

$$
\mathcal{O}(a_k;\mathcal{N})
=
G\left(
\text{stability}(a_k),
\text{redundancy}_{\mathcal{N}}(a_k),
\text{accessibility}_{\mathcal{N}}(a_k),
\text{consistency}_{\mathcal{N}}(a_k)
\right).
$$

This is not a final metric. It states that objectivity varies by aspect and by neighborhood. The acoustic aspect may have high objectivity in a broad physical neighborhood. The source-identity aspect may have high objectivity in a neighborhood containing appropriately equipped perceptual or technological frames. The aesthetic aspect may have lower or more variable objectivity because it depends on frame histories and valuation structures.

This avoids an unhelpful binary. It is not that the chair gets reality wrong and the person gets reality right. Nor is it that every frame receives a private world. Instead, frames share overlapping aspects of events, and objectivity emerges where those aspect-bindings become stable, redundant, accessible, and mutually consistent.

## 9. Relation to Measurement

This paper uses the phrase measurement-like event deliberately. The ice cream truck is not a laboratory measurement apparatus. Nevertheless, its propagation into the room has the same general structure as measurement in Frames Theory: an originating event produces information-bearing physical changes that propagate into candidate receiving frames.

In quantum measurement, the aspect structure may involve possible outcomes, pointer states, environmental records, and observer-accessible readouts. In the room example, the aspect structure involves acoustic, mechanical, semantic, and aesthetic layers. The two cases are not identical, but they share a formal lesson: receiving frames do not bind the total event from nowhere. They bind accessible aspects under their constraints.

This helps clarify the role of candidate binding sites introduced in earlier papers. A candidate binding site is not simply any nearby system. It is a frame whose state-space, coupling, and constraints make it capable of stabilizing some propagated informational structure. Aspect-selective binding makes the qualification explicit: capable of stabilizing which aspects?

For a detector, the relevant aspect might be a pointer outcome. For an environment, it may be scattered photon records. For an observer, it may be a displayed value. For a scientific community, it may be a published result. Each layer binds a different aspect or coarse-graining of the same originating measurement event.

The same reasoning applies in ordinary macroscopic life. The world is saturated with measurement-like propagation. Sounds, light, heat, pressure, chemical traces, inscriptions, memories, and reports continually enter neighborhoods of frames. Each receiving frame filters the incoming structure according to its admissible aspect set.

## 10. Relation to Records and Time

Aspect-selective frame-binding also connects to the Frames Theory account of memory and records. A record is a persistent physical state that allows a frame to reconstruct or constrain a prior event. But records, too, are aspect-selective.

The chair may retain microscopic stress or thermal traces of the sound event, but these are not records of "ice cream truck music" for the chair. They are records, if at all, of mechanical or thermodynamic perturbation. The person may retain a memory of the melody and a prediction that the truck is approaching. A phone recording may retain acoustic waveform data. A written note may retain the semantic report: "The ice cream truck came by at 3 p.m."

All of these records can derive from the same event, but they preserve different aspects.

This suggests an aspect-relative refinement of record notation. If event $E$ leaves a record of aspect $a_k$ in frame $F_i$, write:

$$
E:a_k\longrightarrow R_i(a_k,E).
$$

The accessible past of a frame is then not merely a set of events. It is a set of recorded aspects of events. A person may remember the tune but not the exact truck location. A camera may record the truck visually but not the person's aesthetic reaction. A thermometer may record a temperature change but not the music.

Thus, history is reconstructed from aspect-selective records. This deepens the earlier claim that the past is record-accessible. More precisely, for any frame, the practical past is aspect-record-accessible.

## 11. Relation to Causal Boundaries

Aspect-selective binding also interacts with causal accessibility. A frame cannot bind an aspect that never reaches its causal domain. But causal arrival alone does not guarantee admissibility.

There are therefore two filters.

The first is the **causal filter**:

$$
a_k\in\mathrm{Reach}(F_i)
$$

only if the aspect propagates into the frame's causal or interaction domain.

The second is the **admissibility filter**:

$$
a_k\in\mathcal{A}_i(E)
$$

only if the receiving frame can couple to, stabilize, and process that aspect.

Together:

$$
a_k\in\mathcal{A}_i(E)
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
a_k\in\mathrm{Reach}(F_i)
\text{ and }
a_k\text{ is compatible with }F_i.
$$

This distinction matters in relativistic and cosmological settings, but also in ordinary rooms. The music may reach a sealed box only weakly. It may reach the chair mechanically. It may reach the person acoustically and perceptually. It may reach a neighbor through walls. Each frame's update is constrained first by propagation and then by admissibility.

## 12. Limitations and Open Problems

This paper is conceptual. It does not provide a complete mathematical theory of aspects, nor does it specify a unique decomposition of events into aspect bundles. In a mature version of Frames Theory, aspect decomposition would need to be tied to physical coupling, coarse-graining, information geometry, computational structure, and perhaps category-theoretic maps between state spaces.

The paper also does not claim that semantic or aesthetic aspects are fundamental physical primitives. It treats them as higher-order frame-relative bindings supported by organized physical frames with memory and interpretive constraints. A deeper account would need to explain how such frames emerge from biological, neural, social, and cultural record systems.

The notation $\mathcal{A}_i(E)$ is intentionally schematic. It marks the admissible aspects for a frame, but it does not yet define a quantitative measure of admissibility, binding strength, or semantic depth.

The room example is deliberately simple. Real environments involve many frames and many transformations: walls filter frequencies, air carries pressure, ears perform mechanical transduction, neural systems infer source motion, memory supplies category structure, and culture shapes aesthetic response. A fuller treatment could model these as a chain of transformations across nested subframes.

Finally, aspect-selective binding should be integrated with the existing accounts of Born weights, decoherence, redundant objectivity, causal domains, and records. The present paper provides the conceptual layer, not the final unification.

## 13. Conclusion

Frames Theory treats observation as directed information transfer between bounded informational frames. But information transfer is not a single undifferentiated event. What propagates from an event is structured, and what a receiving frame can bind depends on the frame's own capacities.

The ice cream truck example makes this visible. The chair receives vibrational and thermodynamic aspects of the event. The person receives those aspects plus auditory, semantic, inferential, and aesthetic aspects. Both are updated by the same originating event, but they do not bind the same aspect set.

This paper has called that process aspect-selective frame-binding. A measurement-like event propagates through a neighborhood of frames as a bundle of informational aspects. Each receiving frame filters that bundle according to its coupling channels, state-space, memory, constraints, and type. Exposure is not binding; binding is not interpretation; interpretation is not objectivity.

The result is a more precise account of local informational reality. Frames do not receive events as they are in themselves from nowhere. They receive admissible aspects of events. Shared reality emerges where those aspect-bindings overlap, stabilize, and become mutually consistent across a neighborhood of frames.
