# Time, Memory, and Records in Frames Theory

## Abstract

Time is usually treated either as an external parameter, a coordinate in spacetime, or an experienced flow from past to future. Frames Theory approaches time through bounded informational access. A frame does not possess the universe all at once. It carries records, updates through interactions, and reconstructs histories from stabilized traces. This paper develops the Frames Theory account of time, memory, and records. Time is interpreted as an ordering structure internal to frame update and record accessibility. Memory is stabilized frame-binding to prior information. A record is a persistent physical state that allows a frame or network of frames to reconstruct an earlier event, constraint, or interaction. The arrow of time is not explained by consciousness or by a universal moving present, but by asymmetric record formation, decoherence, entropy increase, and causal propagation. The past is what is record-accessible; the future is what remains open to compatible update; history is a consistency-constrained reconstruction across frames. This paper does not derive thermodynamics, general relativity, or the psychological experience of time. Its goal is to clarify how temporal order and memory arise in a relational informational ontology.

## 1. Introduction

The previous paper extended Frames Theory to cosmological frames and horizon-bounded reality. It argued that no embedded observer can convert the entire universe into an operationally available object. Cosmological description is built from bounded records, causal access, inference, and consistency.

This naturally raises a deeper question:

> What is time for a bounded informational frame?

In ordinary language, time appears to flow. Events happen, become past, leave traces, and are remembered. Physics often treats time differently: as a parameter in dynamics, a coordinate in spacetime, or a dimension in a relativistic manifold. Thermodynamics adds an arrow of increasing entropy. Quantum theory adds state update, decoherence, and records. Cosmology adds horizons and finite observational histories.

Frames Theory does not begin with a single universal flowing present. It begins with frames that receive, store, transform, and reconcile information. From this point of view, time is closely tied to update, memory, and record structure.

The central thesis of this paper is:

> Time, for a frame, is the ordered structure of accessible updates and records; memory is stabilized binding to prior information; history is a consistency-constrained reconstruction across frame networks.

This thesis is not a replacement for the time parameter in physics or for relativistic spacetime. It is an ontological interpretation of temporal experience and temporal description inside Frames Theory.

## 2. Frames as Temporally Bounded Systems

A frame is not merely spatially or informationally bounded. It is temporally bounded as well. It has a finite duration, finite memory, finite update capacity, and finite causal access.

Earlier papers represented a frame schematically as

$$
F_i=(\rho_i,N_i,C_i),
$$

and, in relativistic settings, as

$$
F_i=(\rho_i,N_i,C_i,\mathcal{D}_i).
$$

For the present paper, it is useful to make the record structure explicit:

$$
F_i=(\rho_i,N_i,C_i,\mathcal{D}_i,R_i),
$$

where $R_i$ is the set of records accessible to frame $F_i$.

These records may include internal memory states, environmental traces, measurement logs, written inscriptions, neural states, instrument readouts, fossil records, radiation fields, or cosmological observations. The important feature is not their material form, but their role: they constrain the frame's reconstruction of prior events.

A temporally bounded frame does not access all of spacetime. It accesses a structured set of records within its causal domain. Its temporal orientation is therefore tied to which records it carries and which updates remain possible.

## 3. Records

A record is a persistent physical state that allows a frame to reconstruct or constrain a prior event, interaction, or condition.

In Frames Theory notation, if an event or interaction $E$ leaves a record in frame $F_i$, write

$$
E \longrightarrow R_i(E),
$$

where $R_i(E)$ is the record of $E$ accessible within $F_i$.

A record need not be consciously remembered. It may be a detector state, a scratch on a surface, a photon field, a DNA sequence, a sedimentary layer, a database entry, or the cosmic microwave background. What matters is that the record preserves information in a way that can later constrain a frame's state or inference.

Records have several important properties.

First, they are **physical**. A record is not merely an abstract label. It is carried by a state of some frame or medium.

Second, they are **persistent**. A record must survive long enough to influence later frame states.

Third, they are **accessible**. A record must be available within a frame's causal or informational domain to function as a record for that frame.

Fourth, they are **interpretable**. A record constrains history only relative to a decoding context or model. A mark, memory, or radiation field becomes evidence when a frame can map it to a prior condition.

Fifth, they are **consistency-bearing**. Records in different frames can be compared, reconciled, or found incompatible.

Thus, records are the physical basis of temporal reconstruction.

## 4. Memory as Stabilized Frame-Binding

Memory is a special case of record-bearing. A memory is a stabilized binding between a frame's current state and information from a prior interaction.

In human terms, memory may involve neural encoding, retrieval, and interpretation. But Frames Theory uses the term more broadly. A detector can have a memory state. A laboratory notebook can store memory. A geological formation can preserve memory of earlier conditions. A cosmological radiation field can function as a memory of the early universe.

Memory can be represented schematically as

$$
M_i(E,t_2)=\mathrm{StableBind}_{F_i}\left(R_i(E),t_2\right),
$$

where $M_i(E,t_2)$ is the memory of event $E$ available to frame $F_i$ at a later stage $t_2$.

This notation is not meant as a final mathematical law. It says that memory is record-binding across time within a frame.

Memory has a double role. It preserves information from earlier interactions, and it shapes future updates. A frame with memory does not update as a blank system. Its prior records constrain what it can notice, how it interprets incoming information, and which histories remain consistent.

This is why memory is central to temporal identity. A frame persists through time not by remaining unchanged, but by maintaining enough record continuity to identify itself as an evolving bounded system.

## 5. Temporal Ordering

A frame experiences or represents events as ordered when its records and updates form a consistent sequence.

If event $E_1$ leaves a record that constrains the reception or interpretation of event $E_2$, then $F_i$ may order them as

$$
E_1 \prec_{F_i} E_2.
$$

The symbol $\prec_{F_i}$ denotes frame-relative temporal ordering. It does not necessarily mean that every possible frame has the same ordering. In relativistic settings, spacelike separated events may lack an invariant order. But timelike or causally connected events can be ordered by record dependence.

A causal record relation can be written as

$$
R_i(E_1)\in \mathrm{Access}_{F_i}(E_2)
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
E_1 \prec_{F_i} E_2.
$$

If a record of $E_1$ is available when $E_2$ is processed, then $E_1$ is part of the accessible past of $E_2$ for that frame.

This gives Frames Theory a record-based view of temporal order. Time is not merely a coordinate label. It is the structured ordering of updates, records, and causal accessibility within a frame.

## 6. The Past as Record-Accessible

For a bounded frame, the past is not simply "everything that happened." It is the set of events, conditions, and histories that are accessible through records and consistent reconstruction.

Define the accessible past of a frame schematically as

$$
\mathrm{Past}(F_i)=\{E : R_i(E) \in \mathrm{Access}(F_i)\}.
$$

This is not intended to deny that events may have occurred without leaving accessible records. Rather, it distinguishes occurrence from frame-accessible pastness. For a frame, an event becomes part of its practical past only insofar as the frame can access, infer, or be constrained by records of it.

This distinction matters in cosmology, archaeology, memory, and physics. Many events leave weak, degraded, or ambiguous records. Some leave none accessible to a given frame. Some are inferred statistically rather than directly observed. A frame's past is therefore not a perfect archive. It is a reconstruction from surviving traces.

Frames Theory treats this reconstruction as real but fallible. The past is not invented by the frame. It is constrained by records. But the frame's access to the past is mediated by record survival, interpretation, and consistency.

## 7. The Future as Open Update Space

If the past is record-accessible, the future is update-accessible. It consists of possible state transitions compatible with the frame's current records, causal domain, and physical constraints.

For a frame $F_i$ with current state $\rho_i$ and record set $R_i$, define the compatible future schematically as

$$
\mathrm{Future}(F_i)=\{\rho_i' : \rho_i' \text{ is reachable from } (\rho_i,R_i) \text{ by admissible updates}\}.
$$

This is not a claim that the future is metaphysically unreal. It is a statement about frame access. A frame does not have records of future events in the same way it has records of past events. It has predictions, constraints, amplitudes, probabilities, plans, and dynamical laws.

The asymmetry is informational. The past is constrained by records already bound into the frame. The future is constrained by possibilities not yet bound as records.

This connects directly to the Born-rule papers. Before measurement, a receiving frame may assign a distribution over possible bindings. After binding, the outcome becomes part of the frame's record structure. The transition from possible future to recorded past is a transition in frame-accessible information.

## 8. The Arrow of Time

Frames Theory locates the arrow of time in asymmetric record formation.

In many microscopic laws, the fundamental dynamics are time-reversal symmetric or nearly so. Yet macroscopic life is strongly time-directed. We remember the past, not the future. Records accumulate. Entropy increases. Decoherence stabilizes some histories and suppresses accessible interference between alternatives.

Frames Theory connects these facts through record asymmetry:

$$
\text{arrow of time}
\approx
\text{asymmetric record formation}
+
\text{entropy increase}
+
\text{decoherent stabilization}.
$$

This is a conceptual relation, not a new thermodynamic equation.

A record forms when information about an event is copied, stabilized, or redundantly encoded into other frames. This process typically increases environmental correlations and disperses information into degrees of freedom that are difficult to reverse. Decoherence makes interference between alternative histories inaccessible to ordinary frames. Entropy increase makes record erasure or global reversal overwhelmingly unlikely in practice.

Thus, time has an arrow for frames because records are easier to accumulate than to unmake. The past is record-rich. The future is possibility-rich.

This does not derive the thermodynamic arrow from first principles. It aligns Frames Theory with the standard insight that temporal direction is deeply tied to entropy, boundary conditions, and irreversible record formation.

## 9. Decoherence and Histories

Quantum theory has a natural connection between decoherence and histories. When interactions with the environment suppress interference between alternatives, some sequences of events become stable enough to function as classical histories.

Frames Theory interprets this as record stabilization across time. A history is not merely a sequence of abstract events. It is a sequence that leaves mutually consistent records across frames.

A coarse-grained history might be represented as

$$
H=(E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_n),
$$

with corresponding records

$$
R_i(H)=\{R_i(E_1),R_i(E_2),\ldots,R_i(E_n)\}.
$$

For the history to be stable for frame $F_i$, the records must be mutually consistent:

$$
\mathcal{K}(R_i(E_m),R_i(E_n))\approx 1
$$

where their domains overlap or constrain one another.

This connects Frames Theory to decoherent or consistent histories approaches. Those approaches ask when histories can be assigned probabilities without interference between alternatives. Frames Theory adds its own vocabulary: a history is frame-accessible when records of it are stabilized, decohered, and consistent within the frame network.

The important point is that histories are not simply written into the universe as global narratives. They are reconstructed from records available to frames.

## 10. Forgetting, Erasure, and Record Loss

If time and history depend on records, then forgetting and erasure are physically significant.

A frame may lose access to a prior event if the relevant record decays, is overwritten, becomes inaccessible, or can no longer be interpreted. This does not necessarily mean that the event did not occur. It means that the event no longer participates in that frame's accessible past.

Record loss can be represented schematically as

$$
R_i(E) \longrightarrow \varnothing
$$

relative to frame $F_i$.

In practice, erasure is rarely absolute. Information may persist in the environment, in correlations, or in broader frames even when it is lost locally. A human forgets, but a document remains. A document burns, but thermal traces propagate. A detector resets, but environmental photons may still carry correlations.

This is why record loss is frame-relative. A record may be inaccessible to one frame while still available to another. The accessible past of a frame can shrink, distort, or become ambiguous without implying that all traces vanish globally.

This also clarifies why memory is not infallible. A frame's records can be noisy, corrupted, incomplete, or misleading. Consistency across multiple records and frames is what strengthens historical objectivity.

## 11. Shared Records and Public Time

Individual frames have local memory and local temporal order. Public time emerges when many frames share records and reconcile temporal sequences.

A laboratory clock, a calendar, a database timestamp, and an astronomical ephemeris are all record systems. They help different frames coordinate updates and histories. They do not create time from nothing. They stabilize a shared temporal ordering across a frame network.

Public temporal order emerges from redundant records:

$$
\text{public time}
\approx
\text{shared clocks}
+
\text{redundant records}
+
\text{cross-frame consistency}.
$$

In relativistic settings, public time is limited. Nearby frames can synchronize clocks approximately. Distant frames must account for signal delay, gravitational time dilation, relative motion, and coordinate conventions. There is no single global clock that all frames physically share.

Frames Theory therefore treats public time as an achieved consistency structure. It is objective within domains where clocks, records, and causal relations can be reconciled. It is not an absolute cosmic metronome.

## 12. Cosmological Time and Deep Records

Cosmology provides some of the deepest records available to human frames. The cosmic microwave background, nucleosynthesis abundances, large-scale structure, stellar evolution, geological strata, and biological fossils all function as records of earlier states.

Cosmological time is reconstructed from these records. It is not directly experienced as a single flowing cosmic present. It is inferred through physical models that connect present records to earlier conditions.

For example, the CMB functions as a record of the early universe. Galaxy redshifts function as records of expansion. Element abundances function as records of early nuclear processes. These records are mutually constraining. Together, they produce a coherent cosmological history.

Frames Theory interprets this as large-scale record reconciliation:

$$
H_{\mathrm{cos}}=\mathrm{Reconstruct}\left(R_{\mathrm{CMB}},R_{\mathrm{LSS}},R_{\mathrm{BBN}},R_{\mathrm{stellar}},\ldots\right).
$$

Here $H_{\mathrm{cos}}$ is not a directly observed object. It is a consistency-constrained reconstruction from many record channels.

This connects the present paper to horizon-bounded cosmology. We do not access the full universe. We access records inside our causal patch and reconstruct a history consistent with them.

## 13. Identity and Persistence of Frames

A frame persists through time by maintaining structured continuity across updates. It need not remain physically unchanged. In fact, living, measuring, and observing frames are constantly changing. What persists is an organized record-bearing structure.

For a frame $F_i$ at two stages, $t_1$ and $t_2$, persistence can be expressed as high continuity between record-bearing states:

$$
\mathcal{P}(F_i(t_1),F_i(t_2))=\exp\left(-D(\rho_i(t_1),\rho_i(t_2);R_i)\right),
$$

where $D$ is a schematic distance sensitive to loss of relevant organization and records.

This is not a final identity criterion. It is a way to state the frame-theoretic intuition: identity over time is not sameness of matter, but continuity of organized informational structure.

A person, laboratory, institution, ecosystem, or scientific project persists by preserving enough record structure to remain self-consistent across change. Time, memory, and identity are therefore linked. A frame without memory-like continuity may still exist moment by moment, but it cannot maintain a stable history of itself.

## 15. Relation to Existing Approaches

### 15.1 Thermodynamic Arrow of Time

Thermodynamics connects temporal direction with entropy increase. Frames Theory is compatible with this view. It interprets entropy increase as one reason record formation is asymmetric and reversal is practically inaccessible to ordinary frames.

### 15.2 Decoherent and Consistent Histories

Decoherent histories approaches ask when alternative histories cease to interfere and can be assigned probabilities. Frames Theory is aligned with this project but emphasizes frame-accessible records. A history becomes meaningful for a frame when it leaves stable, mutually consistent records.

### 15.3 Relativity

Relativity denies a universal simultaneity relation. Frames Theory agrees and treats temporal order as local, causal, and record-mediated. Public time is a consistency structure among clocks and records, not a global present.

### 15.4 Memory and Cognitive Science

Human memory involves complex biological and cognitive processes. Frames Theory does not attempt to model those mechanisms here. It abstracts the informational role of memory: stabilized access to prior interactions that constrains future state and identity.

### 15.5 Philosophy of Time

Frames Theory is not straightforwardly presentist, eternalist, or growing-block in the traditional sense. It treats temporal status as frame-relative access: the past is record-accessible, the future is update-open, and global spacetime descriptions are representational tools rather than lived frame states.

## 16. Limitations and Open Problems

This paper is conceptual and interpretive. It does not derive the thermodynamic arrow of time from microscopic dynamics.

It does not provide a complete theory of consciousness, psychological time, subjective duration, attention, or human memory.

It does not replace relativistic spacetime or the time parameter used in physical laws. It interprets temporal ordering and history from within the frame ontology.

It does not solve the problem of time in quantum gravity. A future treatment could ask whether frame-relative records help clarify timeless or relational formulations.

It does not provide a final quantitative measure of record strength, memory stability, or frame persistence. Future work should define such quantities using information theory, entropy, mutual information, decoherence, and consistency metrics.

Finally, it does not make new empirical predictions. Its contribution is to clarify the role of time, memory, and records in Frames Theory.

## 17. Conclusion

Frames Theory begins with bounded informational contexts. Such frames do not possess the universe all at once. They update, bind, remember, forget, and reconstruct.

This paper has argued that time, for a frame, is the ordered structure of accessible updates and records. Memory is stabilized frame-binding to prior information. Records are persistent physical states that allow frames to reconstruct earlier events. The past is record-accessible; the future is update-open; history is consistency-constrained reconstruction across frame networks.

The compact statement is:

$$
\text{time for a frame}=\text{ordered record-accessible update structure}.
$$

This view preserves the usefulness of physical time coordinates while grounding temporal meaning in frame-relative information. It also connects quantum measurement, classical objectivity, relativity, cosmology, and self-reference through a common theme: what becomes real for a frame is what can be bound, recorded, remembered, and reconciled.
