# Frames Theory: A Relational Information Framework for Physical Reality

William J. House

## Abstract

Frames Theory is proposed as a relational informational ontology for physical reality. Its central claim is that physical description should begin not with detached objects observed from nowhere, but with bounded informational frames that exchange, constrain, and reconcile information through interaction. On this view, observation is not a special act of consciousness, but a directed information-transfer relation between frames. Apparent objectivity emerges when many frames become mutually consistent over shared informational neighborhoods.

This paper introduces the basic vocabulary and mathematical scaffold of Frames Theory. A frame is modeled as a bounded informational context with an internal state, accessible neighbors, and consistency constraints. Relations among frames are represented by a directed, weighted information graph with recursive containment structure. The framework is then used to reinterpret several familiar problems: quantum measurement, observer dependence, Wigner's Friend, cosmological horizons, and limits on complete self-description. The goal of this first paper is not to replace quantum mechanics or general relativity, nor to derive their full dynamics, but to formulate a disciplined relational substrate within which existing physical descriptions may be understood as effective frame-relative accounts.

## 1. Introduction

Modern physics contains several unresolved tensions involving observer dependence, measurement, quantum-to-classical emergence, relativistic causality, cosmological horizons, and informational limits. These tensions appear in different languages across quantum foundations, relativity, cosmology, and mathematical logic, but they share a common feature: physical description is always made from within some bounded context of accessible information.

Quantum theory makes this especially visible. The formalism assigns different descriptions to systems depending on what information is available and what interactions have occurred. A system may be described by a superposition relative to one frame while another frame has registered a definite outcome. Relativity also denies a universal external vantage point: simultaneity, causal access, and horizons depend on the structure of spacetime and the location of the observer. Cosmology extends this difficulty further, since some domains may be permanently inaccessible to one another. Self-reference deepens the problem again: observers attempting to describe the universe are themselves physical systems inside the universe they describe.

Frames Theory begins from this shared structure. It proposes that physical reality can be modeled as a recursively embedded network of informational frames. A frame is any bounded context capable of receiving, storing, transforming, or constraining information. A particle interaction, measuring apparatus, biological observer, laboratory, planet, black hole exterior, or cosmological causal patch may all be treated as frames at different scales, provided the relevant informational boundaries and interactions can be specified.

The framework is deliberately modest in its first formulation. It does not claim to be a completed physical theory, a derivation of quantum mechanics, or a replacement for general relativity. Instead, it offers an ontology and formal scaffold: a way of organizing observer dependence, measurement, decoherence, horizons, and self-reference under a common relational vocabulary. Its central hypothesis is that apparent objectivity arises from consistency relations among informational frames, rather than from access to an absolute description independent of all frames.

## 2. Motivating Problems

### 2.1 Measurement and Definite Outcomes

The quantum measurement problem arises from the tension between continuous unitary evolution and the apparent definiteness of observed outcomes. The Schrödinger equation evolves a quantum state deterministically, while measurement appears to select a definite result. Traditional collapse postulates treat this selection as a special event, but the physical status of collapse remains unclear.

Decoherence explains part of the difficulty by showing how environmental entanglement suppresses interference between macroscopic alternatives. It explains why certain outcomes become stable and effectively classical relative to an environment. However, decoherence by itself does not settle the ontology of outcome selection. It does not say, in a frame-independent way, what has become real for all possible observers.

Frames Theory recasts the problem. A measurement is not a metaphysically special act, but an information-transfer event in which one frame becomes correlated with another. A definite outcome is a stabilized state relative to the receiving frame and its consistency constraints. The question is therefore not, "When does the universal wavefunction collapse?" but, "How do frames acquire and reconcile information such that stable outcomes become mutually consistent?"

### 2.2 Observer Dependence and Wigner's Friend

Wigner's Friend illustrates the difficulty of assigning a single absolute description to a quantum event. Inside a sealed laboratory, the friend measures a system and records a definite outcome. Outside the laboratory, Wigner may describe the combined system of particle, apparatus, and friend as a coherent superposition. Both descriptions can be generated by legitimate applications of quantum theory, but they appear to assign different statuses to the same event.

This is not merely a paradox about human observers. In modern quantum information language, observation does not require consciousness. Any physical interaction that establishes a durable correlation can function as an observation. A detector, environment, or thermodynamic record may serve the relevant role. The important structure is not mentality, but information exchange.

Frames Theory treats the friend and Wigner as distinct informational frames. The friend has information internal to the laboratory frame; Wigner has information about the laboratory as an external system. Their descriptions need not be identical before interaction because they are conditioned on different informational relations. When Wigner later interacts with the laboratory, consistency constraints must reconcile the frames over their shared informational neighborhood.

### 2.3 Cosmological Horizons

General relativity introduces causal limits that are not merely practical. Cosmological horizons, black hole horizons, and causal patch boundaries restrict which events can exchange information. Regions beyond a cosmic event horizon may never become observationally accessible to a given frame. In such cases, the idea of a single operationally available global reality becomes strained.

Frames Theory interprets such limits as weak or absent connectivity between informational frames. If no information can propagate between two domains, then their relation cannot be established by direct observation or later reconciliation. They may still be parts of a larger mathematical structure, but they do not share a common operational reality from the perspective of either bounded frame.

This suggests a cautious cosmological extension: reality may be only partially constructible from inside the universe. There may be no complete internal description available to any embedded observer, only overlapping relational patches connected by finite information transfer.

### 2.4 Self-Reference and Incomplete Description

Physical observers are not outside the universe. They are subsystems within it. Any observer attempting to model the whole must model a system that includes the observer, the observer's records, and the act of modeling itself. This creates self-referential structure.

The analogy with Gödel incompleteness should be used carefully. Gödel's theorems apply to sufficiently expressive formal systems, not automatically to physical reality. Still, the analogy is suggestive: systems rich enough to encode descriptions of themselves may face structural limits on complete self-description. Related limits also arise from finite information capacity, quantum uncertainty, computational irreducibility, and horizons.

Frames Theory does not claim to prove that a final theory is impossible. It instead treats self-reference as another reason to avoid assuming a God's-eye description as physically primary. A theory of embedded observers should explain how local, partial, and mutually constrained descriptions arise inside the system itself.

## 3. Core Ontology of Frames Theory

### 3.1 Primitive Structure

The primitive structure of Frames Theory is information transfer between bounded contexts. A context becomes a frame when it has an informational state, possible relations to other frames, and constraints governing which state-relations are compatible.

This choice makes information exchange primary, while allowing causality, measurement, consistency, and apparent objectivity to be modeled as derived or structured features of frame interaction. Causality constrains which information transfers are possible. Observation is a realized information transfer. Consistency measures whether frames can be jointly reconciled over shared information. Objectivity is the limiting behavior of many frames maintaining stable mutual consistency.

### 3.2 Definition 1: Frame

A frame $F_i$ is a bounded informational context characterized by:

$$
F_i = (\rho_i, N_i, C_i)
$$

where:

- $\rho_i$ is the informational state of the frame.
- $N_i$ is the set of neighboring or accessible frames.
- $C_i$ is the set of consistency constraints governing admissible relations.

The state $\rho_i$ may represent a quantum density matrix, a classical probability distribution, or a more general informational state, depending on the domain being modeled. The theory does not require all frames to have the same internal representation at every scale. What matters is that each frame has a bounded state and a rule-governed relation to other frames.

Frames are not restricted by scale. A microscopic interaction, a detector, a laboratory, an observer, an ecosystem, a black hole exterior, or a cosmological causal patch may be modeled as a frame when its informational boundary is relevant. From the Planck scale to the cosmological scale, the frame concept is intended to track informational boundedness rather than size or complexity.

### 3.3 Definition 2: Observation

Observation is directed information transfer between frames:

$$
O_{ij}: F_i \rightarrow F_j
$$

The notation means that frame $F_j$ receives information from frame $F_i$. Observation is therefore not defined as conscious awareness. It is a physical relation in which the receiving frame is updated by information from another frame.

In this terminology, measurement is a special case of observation: an information-transfer event that creates a stable record within the receiving frame and its environment. A photon scattering event, detector click, thermodynamic trace, or human perceptual report may all be observations in this broader sense when they establish a relevant informational correlation.

### 3.4 Definition 3: Containment

Containment defines recursive embedding among frames. If frame $F_i$ is contained within, constrained by, or embedded in frame $F_j$, write:

$$
F_i \subseteq F_j
$$

For more general embedding relations, write:

$$
i \preceq j
$$

Containment need not form a simple tree. A frame may participate in multiple overlapping contexts. A laboratory may contain a detector, while also being contained in a planetary environment and coupled to an observer community. A quantum subsystem may be embedded in several relevant decompositions depending on the interaction being analyzed. For this reason, recursive containment may be represented by a partial order, directed acyclic structure, or hypergraph rather than a strict hierarchy.

### 3.5 Definition 4: Consistency

Consistency measures the degree to which two frames can be jointly reconciled over shared information. Let the shared informational neighborhood of frames $a$ and $b$ be:

$$
\Omega(a,b)=N(a)\cap N(b)
$$

Then define an inter-frame consistency functional:

$$
\mathcal{K}(a,b)=\exp\left(-D(\rho_a^{\Omega},\rho_b^{\Omega})\right)
$$

where $D$ is an information distance between the restrictions of the two frame states to their shared neighborhood. Depending on the formal setting, $D$ may be instantiated as trace distance, relative entropy, KL divergence, or another suitable measure.

When $\mathcal{K}(a,b)\approx 1$, the frames are highly compatible over their shared information. When $\mathcal{K}(a,b)\approx 0$, the frames are effectively incompatible or weakly reconcilable. This does not imply that one frame is simply false. It means that the information available to the frames does not yet support a common stabilized description.

### 3.6 Definition 5: Reality

Reality is the maximal consistency-preserving informational network:

$$
\mathcal{R}=(V,E,W,\rho,\preceq,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{K})
$$

where:

- $V$ is the set of frames.
- $E$ is the set of information-transfer links.
- $W$ is the set of edge weights.
- $\rho$ assigns informational states to frames.
- $\preceq$ represents containment or embedding relations.
- $\mathcal{F}$ represents update dynamics.
- $\mathcal{K}$ represents inter-frame consistency.

In plain terms, reality is not first modeled as a collection of frame-independent objects. It is modeled as a structured network of bounded informational contexts whose apparent objectivity emerges from stable consistency across interactions.

## 4. Mathematical Skeleton

### 4.1 Directed Information Graph

Let the frame network be represented by a directed weighted graph:

$$
G=(V,E,W)
$$

Here $V$ is the set of frames, $E$ is the set of directed information-transfer relations, and $W$ assigns weights to those relations. An edge $i\rightarrow j$ means that frame $j$ receives information from frame $i$.

Each edge has a weight:

$$
w_{ij}\in[0,1]
$$

The weight $w_{ij}$ may represent coupling strength, information fidelity, causal accessibility, or effective reliability of the transfer. A value near $1$ indicates strong informational access. A value near $0$ indicates weak, noisy, or absent access.

### 4.2 Frame States

Each frame $F_i$ has an internal informational state $\rho_i$. In quantum contexts, $\rho_i$ may be a density operator. In classical contexts, it may be a probability distribution. In more general settings, it may be an abstract state in an information space.

The framework intentionally allows different state descriptions at different levels, but this flexibility is also a source of future work. A mature version of Frames Theory must specify when different state spaces are equivalent, when one emerges from another, and how transitions between descriptions are governed.

### 4.3 Information Transfer

Information transfer updates the receiving frame. A schematic update rule is:

$$
\rho_j(t+1)=\mathcal{F}_j\left(\rho_j(t),\{w_{ij}\rho_i(t):i\rightarrow j\}\right)
$$

This says that frame $j$ evolves from its prior state together with weighted information received from frames connected to it. The function $\mathcal{F}_j$ is intentionally left abstract in this first formulation. In different physical regimes, it may correspond to quantum evolution, Bayesian updating, thermodynamic relaxation, decoherence dynamics, or another domain-specific update rule.

The open problem is to identify general constraints on $\mathcal{F}_j$ that preserve consistency, respect causal structure, and reproduce known physics in appropriate limits.

### 4.4 Consistency Metric

The consistency functional $\mathcal{K}(a,b)$ measures compatibility between frames over shared information. The general form is:

$$
\mathcal{K}(a,b)=\exp\left(-D(\rho_a^{\Omega},\rho_b^{\Omega})\right)
$$

The exponential form is useful because it maps zero distance to maximal consistency and large distance to low consistency. This is a scaffold, not a final law. A complete theory must specify the correct distance measure for each class of frame state and explain how consistency propagates through larger networks.

### 4.5 Recursive Containment

Recursive containment is represented by the relation $\preceq$. If $i\preceq k$, then frame $i$ is contained within, constrained by, or embedded in frame $k$. A containing frame may impose boundary conditions or admissibility constraints on its contained frames.

Containment can also be represented by a child-set relation:

$$
C_k\subseteq V
$$

where $C_k$ is the set of frames constrained by enclosing frame $k$. This representation allows a frame to organize many subframes without requiring that the full network be a simple hierarchy.

## 5. Relation to Existing Physics

Frames Theory is not intended to discard existing physics. Its purpose is to provide a relational ontology in which familiar structures can be interpreted as effective frame-relative descriptions.

| Existing Concept | Frames Theory Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Measurement | Frame-link stabilization |
| Observer | Informational frame |
| Decoherence | Environmental consistency propagation |
| Collapse | Local state reconciliation |
| Classicality | High-consistency macroscopic subnet |
| Spacetime locality | Stable information topology |
| Cosmological horizon | Weak or absent frame connectivity |

In quantum mechanics, a measurement correlates a system with a measuring apparatus. In Frames Theory, this is an information-transfer relation that changes the receiving frame's state. In decoherence, the environment stabilizes certain records by distributing correlations across many degrees of freedom. In Frames Theory, this is consistency propagation across a large neighboring frame network.

Classicality then corresponds to a regime in which many frames maintain high mutual consistency. A chair, a detector reading, or a laboratory record appears objective because many interacting frames can access and reconcile the same information. Objectivity is not denied; it is reinterpreted as a stable network property rather than an assumption of frame-independent access.

## 6. Wigner's Friend Revisited

In the Wigner's Friend scenario, the friend inside a laboratory measures a quantum system and records a definite result. Relative to the friend's frame, the outcome is stabilized. Relative to Wigner outside the laboratory, the combined system may still be described as a coherent superposition if Wigner has not interacted with it.

Frames Theory treats these as different but legitimate frame-relative descriptions. The friend has an internal information link to the measured system. Wigner has a different relation to the laboratory as a whole. The apparent contradiction arises when one demands a single absolute description prior to specifying the relevant frame relations.

When Wigner interacts with the laboratory, a new information-transfer event occurs. The two descriptions must then become consistent over their shared informational neighborhood. This reconciliation need not be described as universal collapse. It can be described as the formation of higher-order consistency between previously distinct frames.

This example shows the intended use of Frames Theory. The framework does not eliminate the mathematical formalism of quantum mechanics. It clarifies the ontology of description: facts are not free-floating absolutes, but stabilized relations within and between informational frames.

## 7. Cosmological Extensions

The same logic may extend to cosmology, though more cautiously. Cosmological horizons create domains that may never exchange information. If two regions cannot interact, then no shared observational frame can reconcile their descriptions. They may be mathematically included in a larger model, but not operationally accessed from within either bounded domain.

This suggests that global reality may not be fully constructible by any embedded frame. Instead, physical reality may consist of overlapping patches of high consistency separated by horizons, weak connectivity, or incompatible state spaces. Attempts to map one inaccessible domain into another may be lossy, approximate, or non-isomorphic.

Such ideas resonate with causal patch cosmology, black hole complementarity, holography, and relational interpretations of quantum mechanics. However, in this paper they remain extensions rather than central claims. The primary claim is not that Frames Theory solves cosmology, but that cosmological horizons provide another domain in which informational boundedness is physically significant.

## 8. Limitations and Open Problems

This first formulation leaves several major problems unresolved.

First, Frames Theory does not yet derive the Born rule or explain quantum probabilities from first principles. It can describe frame-relative stabilization, but it does not yet explain why outcome probabilities take the specific form used in quantum mechanics.

Second, it does not derive general relativity, Lorentz symmetry, or Einstein's field equations. It suggests that spacetime locality may be related to stable information topology, but this remains a research direction rather than an established result.

Third, the update rule $\mathcal{F}_j$ is schematic. A complete theory must specify its form, domain of validity, and relation to known dynamical laws.

Fourth, consistency propagation is not yet fully formalized. The functional $\mathcal{K}$ provides a starting measure of compatibility, but the dynamics by which consistency spreads through large frame networks remain open.

Fifth, the self-reference and incompleteness discussion is suggestive rather than proven. Any rigorous connection to mathematical incompleteness would require a precise formal encoding of frames, observers, and self-modeling.

Finally, the framework does not yet make new experimental predictions. Its present value is conceptual and structural: it organizes several foundational problems under a common relational information language. Future work must show whether this organization produces new mathematics, new derivations, or testable differences from existing frameworks.

## 9. Conclusion

Frames Theory proposes that physical reality can be modeled as a recursively embedded network of informational frames. Observation is directed information transfer. Measurement is stabilized information transfer. Objectivity emerges from consistency across shared informational neighborhoods. Horizons and self-reference reveal limits on globally complete description.

The framework is intentionally incomplete. Its purpose at this stage is to establish a disciplined ontology and mathematical scaffold, not to replace established physical theories. If successful, Frames Theory may provide a higher-order relational substrate within which quantum measurement, decoherence, observer dependence, causal horizons, and incomplete self-description can be understood as manifestations of a common structure: bounded frames exchanging and reconciling information.

The next steps are to sharpen the update dynamics, identify the appropriate state spaces and distance measures, formalize consistency propagation, and determine whether known physics can be recovered as limiting behavior of the frame network.
