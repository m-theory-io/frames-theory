# Entanglement, Nonlocality, and No-Signaling in Frames Theory

## Abstract

Entanglement produces correlations that cannot be explained by local hidden variables, yet quantum theory also forbids controllable faster-than-light signaling. This combination is one of the central interpretive pressures in quantum foundations. Frames Theory offers a relational informational account of the tension. Entanglement is interpreted as a distributed binding structure generated by a joint source frame and later actualized through local receiving frames. Measurement outcomes become definite only relative to the frames that bind to them, while the correlations between distant outcomes are constrained by the prior joint state and reconciled when records are compared through ordinary causal channels. Nonlocality is therefore not a superluminal signal passing between distant measurement events. It is the failure of separable frame-local descriptions to exhaust the correlation structure of an entangled system. No-signaling is preserved because local reduced states remain independent of distant measurement choices until information about those choices is received through causal propagation. This paper develops the Frames Theory account of entanglement, Bell-type correlations, no-signaling, and delayed reconciliation across frame networks.

## 1. Introduction

The previous papers in this sequence developed Frames Theory as a relational informational framework for quantum measurement, state update, Born-rule weights, and classical objectivity. The next natural question is entanglement at a distance.

Entangled systems challenge any theory that tries to treat physical properties as independently possessed by isolated local systems. When two particles are prepared in an entangled state and measured far apart, the resulting correlations can violate Bell inequalities. The correlations are stronger than any local hidden-variable model permits. Yet the same correlations cannot be used to send controllable faster-than-light messages.

This gives rise to a familiar puzzle:

> How can quantum correlations be nonlocal without allowing superluminal signaling?

Frames Theory approaches this puzzle by distinguishing three ideas.

**Distributed entanglement.** A joint generating frame can prepare systems whose state is not decomposable into independent local states.

**Local frame-binding.** Each measurement outcome becomes definite only when a local receiving frame binds to an outcome-relative state.

**Record reconciliation.** The relation between distant outcomes becomes available to a broader frame only when records are later compared through ordinary causal propagation.

The central thesis of this paper is:

> Entanglement is distributed frame-binding potential; nonlocality is cross-frame correlation without separable local completion; no-signaling is preserved because usable information propagates only through causal frame channels.

This thesis does not modify quantum predictions. It offers an interpretation of what entangled correlations mean inside Frames Theory. The goal is to preserve the empirical force of Bell nonlocality while avoiding the misleading image of a signal leaping from one measurement station to another.

## 2. Entanglement as Distributed Frame Structure

Consider two systems $A$ and $B$ prepared by a common generating frame $G$ in an entangled state. A simple example is the Bell state

$$
|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB}=\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\left(|0\rangle_A|0\rangle_B+|1\rangle_A|1\rangle_B\right).
$$

This state cannot be written as a product of separate states for $A$ and $B$:

$$
|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB}
\ne
|\psi\rangle_A|\phi\rangle_B.
$$

In standard quantum language, the systems are entangled. In Frames Theory, this means that the generating frame has produced a distributed informational structure whose outcome constraints are not exhausted by either subsystem alone.

The reduced state of either subsystem is mixed:

$$
\rho_A=\mathrm{Tr}_B\left(|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+|\right)=\frac{1}{2}I,
$$

and likewise

$$
\rho_B=\frac{1}{2}I.
$$

The local reduced state contains no definite value for the correlated outcome. It also contains no information by itself about which measurement will be chosen at the distant wing. The correlation lives in the joint structure, not in either local reduced state alone.

Frames Theory interprets this as distributed frame-binding potential. The source frame $G$ does not assign two already definite local facts, one to $A$ and one to $B$. Rather, it generates a joint state that constrains how later bindings at local receiving frames can be reconciled.

This is why entanglement is not merely a lack of knowledge. The mixed state $\rho_A$ is not just ignorance about a hidden local value possessed by $A$. It is the state available to a local frame whose access is restricted to $A$. The missing information is not hidden inside $A$ as a classical property. It belongs to the nonseparable relation between $A$ and $B$.

## 3. Local Measurement and Frame-Binding

Suppose system $A$ is measured by receiving frame $F_A$, and system $B$ is measured by receiving frame $F_B$. The measurement settings are labeled $x$ and $y$, and the outcomes are labeled $a$ and $b$.

Frames Theory treats each local measurement as a local binding event. At station $A$, the receiving frame $F_A$ binds to an outcome-relative state determined by its local interaction with $A$ and its setting $x$. At station $B$, the receiving frame $F_B$ binds to an outcome-relative state determined by its local interaction with $B$ and its setting $y$.

The local binding at $A$ is not caused by a signal from $B$. Likewise, the local binding at $B$ is not caused by a signal from $A$. Each event occurs through local coupling between the system and its receiving frame.

Yet the outcomes are not independent. The joint probabilities are given by the quantum state and measurement settings:

$$
P(a,b|x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}\left[(M^x_a\otimes N^y_b)\rho_{AB}\right],
$$

where $M^x_a$ and $N^y_b$ are the measurement operators associated with the two local settings.

This equation is important because it keeps the two aspects of the situation together. The measurement operators are local to their receiving frames, but the probability rule uses the joint state $\rho_{AB}$. The outcomes are locally actualized, but their correlation is jointly constrained.

In Frames Theory language, local actualization and joint consistency are distinct. A frame can bind locally, while the relation between distant bindings is only later available to a broader comparison frame.

## 4. Bell Nonlocality

Bell's theorem shows that no theory of local hidden variables can reproduce all quantum predictions. In a typical Bell experiment, one assumes that outcomes are determined by local settings and some shared hidden variable $\lambda$:

$$
a=a(x,\lambda),
\qquad
b=b(y,\lambda).
$$

Together with suitable statistical assumptions, this leads to Bell inequalities. Quantum mechanics violates these inequalities. For the CHSH scenario, one defines a combination of correlations

$$
S=E(x,y)+E(x,y')+E(x',y)-E(x',y'),
$$

and local hidden-variable theories require

$$
|S|\leq 2.
$$

Quantum mechanics allows

$$
|S|\leq 2\sqrt 2,
$$

and experiments violate the classical bound.

Frames Theory accepts this result. It does not try to restore local hidden variables. The lesson of Bell nonlocality is that entangled systems cannot be understood as carrying a complete set of preexisting local values that are merely revealed by measurement.

Instead, Frames Theory interprets Bell violation as evidence that the relevant informational structure is not separable into independent local frame states prior to measurement. The source frame prepares a distributed state. The local receiving frames actualize outcomes through local interactions. The joint pattern of those outcomes is constrained by the nonseparable state.

This gives a careful meaning to nonlocality. Nonlocality does not mean that a usable message travels from one measurement station to the other. It means that the complete correlation structure cannot be represented as a product of independent local facts plus a shared classical variable.

In short:

$$
\text{Bell nonlocality}
\ne
\text{superluminal signaling}.
$$

Rather,

$$
\text{Bell nonlocality}=\text{failure of separable local completion}.
$$

Frames Theory therefore treats nonlocality as a constraint on descriptions, not as an ordinary signal process between distant events.

## 5. No-Signaling as Local Accessibility Constraint

Quantum theory forbids controllable faster-than-light signaling. In the bipartite case, this means that the marginal probability for Alice's outcome does not depend on Bob's measurement setting:

$$
P(a|x)=\sum_b P(a,b|x,y)
$$

is independent of $y$. Similarly,

$$
P(b|y)=\sum_a P(a,b|x,y)
$$

is independent of $x$.

In density-matrix language, if Alice has access only to subsystem $A$, then her local state is

$$
\rho_A=\mathrm{Tr}_B(\rho_{AB}).
$$

Bob's local choice of measurement setting does not by itself change the state available inside Alice's frame. Alice's receiving frame can bind to a local outcome, but she cannot infer Bob's setting or outcome from her local result alone.

Frames Theory interprets no-signaling as an accessibility constraint:

> A frame cannot use correlations whose comparison records have not yet entered its informational domain.

The joint correlation between $A$ and $B$ may be real as a constraint on the distributed state. But a local frame has access only to its local reduced state and local records. Until a causal signal carrying Bob's setting and outcome reaches Alice, Bob's record is outside Alice's accessible frame.

This is why entanglement does not become a communication channel. The correlation structure is nonseparable, but usable information requires record propagation. Record propagation is constrained by ordinary causal structure.

No-signaling is therefore not an extra patch added to Frames Theory. It follows from the distinction between joint state constraints and local frame accessibility.

## 6. Delayed Reconciliation of Records

In an entanglement experiment, the distant outcomes become meaningful as a correlated pair only when they are brought into a common comparison frame. This may happen when Alice and Bob later exchange classical messages, upload records to a shared database, or compare logs in a laboratory frame.

Before reconciliation, Alice's frame contains her setting and outcome:

$$
F_A:\quad (x,a).
$$

Bob's frame contains his setting and outcome:

$$
F_B:\quad (y,b).
$$

A later comparison frame $F_C$ receives both records:

$$
F_C:\quad (x,a;y,b).
$$

Only in $F_C$ is the joint correlation directly available as a record. The comparison frame can estimate quantities such as

$$
E(x,y)=\sum_{a,b}ab\,P(a,b|x,y).
$$

This delayed reconciliation is crucial. It prevents the appearance of a contradiction between local randomness and joint order. Locally, each side sees outcomes distributed according to its reduced state. Jointly, after records are compared, the correlations reveal the structure of the entangled source.

The entangled state therefore constrains a family of possible local bindings, while the empirical nonlocal correlation appears only through later cross-frame record comparison.

Frames Theory can summarize the process as follows:

1. **Generation:** a source frame prepares a nonseparable joint state.
2. **Distribution:** subsystems propagate to spacelike separated receiving frames.
3. **Local binding:** each receiving frame actualizes a local outcome.
4. **Local accessibility:** each frame has access only to its local setting and outcome.
5. **Causal communication:** records propagate through ordinary channels.
6. **Reconciliation:** a comparison frame constructs the joint correlation record.

At no stage is a controllable superluminal signal required.

## 7. Spacelike Separation and Frame Ordering

Bell experiments are often arranged so that the two measurement events are spacelike separated. In such cases, different inertial frames may disagree about which measurement happened first. A satisfactory interpretation should not depend on a preferred temporal ordering between the two measurements.

Frames Theory handles this by avoiding the claim that one distant measurement causes the other through an ordinary time-ordered signal. The local binding at $A$ is local to $F_A$. The local binding at $B$ is local to $F_B$. Their correlation is inherited from the joint state prepared by the source frame and later reconciled in a comparison frame.

There is therefore no need to say that Alice's measurement instantaneously changes Bob's local reality, or that Bob's measurement instantaneously changes Alice's local reality. What changes locally is each receiving frame's accessible state. What is nonlocal is the structure of the joint constraints, not a causal message exchanged between the two stations.

This distinction helps Frames Theory remain compatible with relativistic causal ordering at the operational level. Usable information still travels through causal channels. The theory does not introduce a preferred frame of superluminal communication.

This does not mean that relativistic quantum theory is fully addressed here. A complete account would need to handle fields, local algebras, causal diamonds, and observer-dependent horizons. The present paper makes a narrower claim: Bell-type entanglement can be interpreted without treating nonlocal correlation as superluminal signaling.

## 8. Entanglement Swapping and Networked Binding

Entanglement is not limited to simple two-particle cases. In entanglement swapping, two systems that have never directly interacted can become entangled through a joint measurement on their partners. A simplified initial structure is

$$
|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB}\otimes |\Phi^+\rangle_{CD}.
$$

A Bell-state measurement on $B$ and $C$ can condition the state of $A$ and $D$ into an entangled relation. In ordinary language, entanglement has been swapped from the pairs $(A,B)$ and $(C,D)$ to the pair $(A,D)$.

Frames Theory interprets this as networked redistribution of binding constraints. The measurement frame for $B$ and $C$ binds to a joint outcome. That outcome conditions how the records of $A$ and $D$ can later be reconciled. The entanglement relation is not a material thread connecting particles. It is a constraint structure over possible frame-bindings, updated by measurement and record propagation.

This is another case where no-signaling remains intact. The distant systems $A$ and $D$ do not gain usable information about the Bell-state measurement until the relevant record is communicated. The joint correlation becomes available only when the conditioning record enters the appropriate comparison frame.

Entanglement swapping therefore supports the same general lesson: quantum correlations are relations among frame-accessible records constrained by joint states and measurement histories. They are not signals sent through hidden channels.

## 9. Relation to the Prior Papers

This paper extends the earlier sequence in a specific direction.

The first paper introduced frames as bounded informational contexts and observation as directed information transfer.

The second paper interpreted the Born rule as a frame-binding measure.

The third paper interpreted projection, partial trace, and decoherence as frame-relative state-update operations.

The fourth paper argued that the Born measure is selected by consistency constraints over frame refinements and containment.

The fifth paper explained classical objectivity as redundant frame-binding across a recursive information network.

The present paper applies those ideas to entanglement and Bell-type nonlocality. It shows how entangled correlations can be nonseparable without becoming usable superluminal signals.

The resulting sequence is:

$$
\text{frames}
\longrightarrow
\text{binding measure}
\longrightarrow
\text{state update}
\longrightarrow
\text{measure derivation}
\longrightarrow
\text{classical objectivity}
\longrightarrow
\text{entanglement and no-signaling}.
$$

## 10. Relation to Existing Approaches

### 10.1 Copenhagen and Operational Views

Operational approaches emphasize that quantum theory predicts correlations between measurement outcomes and forbids faster-than-light signaling. Frames Theory agrees with these operational facts. Its contribution is to give a relational informational account of why local outcomes and joint correlations belong to different frame-access domains.

### 10.2 Everettian Interpretations

Everettian interpretations treat entangled measurements through global unitary evolution and branching. Frames Theory shares the rejection of universal collapse, but it does not make branches the basic explanatory unit. It centers receiving frames, local bindings, record propagation, and reconciliation.

### 10.3 Relational Quantum Mechanics

Relational Quantum Mechanics treats physical properties as relative to interacting systems. Frames Theory is close in spirit, but adds explicit machinery for generating frames, candidate binding sites, accessibility constraints, and later comparison frames.

### 10.4 QBism

QBism treats quantum states as expressions of an agent's personal expectations about experience. Frames Theory is not agent-centered. It treats detectors, apparatuses, environments, and databases as physical frames. No-signaling is therefore not only a constraint on agent expectations, but a constraint on what records can enter a frame's accessible domain.

### 10.5 Bohmian Mechanics and Hidden-Variable Theories

Bohmian mechanics accepts nonlocal dynamics in order to reproduce quantum correlations. Frames Theory does not introduce hidden variables or a guiding equation. It accepts Bell's rejection of separable local completion and interprets the resulting nonlocality as a feature of joint frame constraints rather than as a controllable signal.

### 10.6 Objective Collapse Theories

Objective collapse theories modify quantum dynamics to produce real physical collapses. Frames Theory does not add collapse dynamics. Local outcomes are frame-relative bindings, and joint correlations are reconciled through record comparison.

## 11. Limitations and Open Problems

This paper is interpretive and structural. It does not change the quantum predictions for Bell experiments, entanglement swapping, or no-signaling.

It does not provide a full relativistic quantum field theoretic treatment. A later paper should formulate frames in terms of local regions, causal domains, field algebras, and horizon-limited accessibility.

It does not solve every problem associated with Lorentz invariance and state update. It avoids preferred temporal ordering in the Bell case by treating local binding and later reconciliation separately, but a complete theory must handle general spacetime settings.

It does not derive Tsirelson's bound from Frames Theory principles. It uses the standard quantum bound as part of the accepted formalism. A future technical treatment might ask whether frame-consistency constraints can illuminate why quantum correlations stop at $2\sqrt 2$ rather than reaching the algebraic maximum.

It does not offer a hidden-variable account of individual outcomes. Frames Theory interprets local outcomes as actualized bindings, not as the revelation of preexisting local values.

Finally, this paper does not claim that entanglement is identical to frame-binding in every sense. Entanglement is the quantum substrate of many binding relations. Stable frame-binding also depends on accessibility, decoherence, propagation, and consistency across frame networks.

## 12. Conclusion

Entanglement forces a theory of quantum measurement to give up separable local completion. No-signaling forces it to avoid treating nonlocal correlation as a controllable message. Frames Theory accommodates both constraints by distinguishing joint state structure, local binding, and causal record propagation.

An entangled source frame generates a distributed state. Local receiving frames bind to outcome-relative states through local interactions. The outcomes are not independent, because their joint probabilities are constrained by the nonseparable state. But neither frame can use the correlation to signal, because usable information requires access to comparison records, and those records propagate through ordinary causal channels.

The resulting view is compact:

$$
\text{entanglement}=\text{distributed binding constraint},
$$

$$
\text{nonlocality}=\text{failure of separable local completion},
$$

$$
\text{no-signaling}=\text{causal limitation on frame-accessible records}.
$$

This preserves the empirical content of quantum mechanics while giving Frames Theory a clear account of one of its sharpest interpretive tests: quantum correlations can be nonlocal without becoming signals.
